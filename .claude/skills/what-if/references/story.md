# What If story and research

Each video answers one imaginative question with an understandable chain of consequences. The visuals may be dramatic; factual claims stay grounded and qualified.

## Pick the question

Good What If questions change one clear variable and produce a visible result:

- What if Earth stopped rotating?
- What if the Moon disappeared?
- What if every ocean suddenly drained?
- What if humans could see infrared light?

Prefer questions where a real physical, biological or engineering mechanism can explain the outcome. Avoid miracle cures, money promises, graphic harm and claims that could be mistaken for practical emergency advice.

## Research before scripting

Use primary and authoritative sources: scientific papers, universities, space agencies, meteorological or geological agencies, and original datasets. Record the URL, publisher, publication date and the claim it supports in `out/<slug>/sources.md`.

For each claim, decide whether it is:

1. established science;
2. a model-based estimate with assumptions; or
3. a story inference added to make the scenario visual.

State uncertainty in the narration. Do not invent exact numbers, timelines or quotes. If sources disagree, explain the range briefly or leave the claim out. Cite sources in the description and production notes.

## Seven-beat arc

| Beat | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Hook | Ask the question and show the first impossible image | “What if Earth stopped spinning right now?” |
| 2. Setup | Establish the one change and the ordinary baseline | A rotating globe, a clock and a person standing still |
| 3. Trigger | Show the instant the rule changes | The globe locks; the atmosphere keeps moving |
| 4. First effect | Explain the closest consequence | Air and loose objects keep their momentum |
| 5. Escalation | Build one cause into the next | Wind, ocean motion, coastlines |
| 6. Largest consequence | Show the biggest grounded result, with uncertainty stated | Extreme regional effects, not an unsupported universal outcome |
| 7. Takeaway | Give the short answer or a final question | “Rotation is part of what makes Earth livable.” |

For longer videos, add character-scale examples, compare timescales, and return to the original question. Do not pad runtime by repeating the same consequence.

## Script and timing

- Default Short: exactly 60 seconds; narration usually 80–125 words. Change the runtime only when the user asks.
- Write for speech, not the page: use contractions, varied sentence lengths, concrete words and one main idea per breath.
- Keep the narrator curious, warm and conversational. Put suspense in the question, facts and visual reveal; avoid making every line sound like a trailer or horror voiceover.
- Check pronunciation of scientific terms, names, units and numbers. Spell out or phoneticize only where TTS needs help; preserve correct spelling in captions.
- Mark pauses, stressed words and breath points. Let an image or sound beat carry a pause instead of filling every second with speech.
- Preview the actual TTS voice before timing the edit. Rewrite any phrase that sounds stiff, robotic, breathless or difficult to say aloud; do not try to repair poor phrasing by pitch-shifting the completed voice.
- Leave room for visual reveals and captions. Read the final narration at the actual TTS pace before storyboarding.
- Make every narration line correspond to a visible action, diagram, map, number or character reaction.
- Let captions emphasize key words and quantities; never require viewers to read a paragraph.
- End with an answer, limitation or memorable question, not a generic engagement plea.

Write `out/<slug>/story.md` with the question, one-sentence answer, source-backed narration, the seven beats, visual notes, uncertainty language, title and description. Ask a science-literate viewer whether the causal chain makes sense before animating.
